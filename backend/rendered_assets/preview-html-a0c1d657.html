Video Script

**Production notes:** Use a calm, steady pace with short pauses. Include captions for all narration. Keep on-screen text brief, high-contrast, and large enough for mobile viewing. Use gentle visuals, no flashing effects, and soft background music or natural sound at low volume. When showing a shift from “alarm” to “recovery,” do not rely on color alone; also use labels, icons, motion, and/or shape changes.

| Time | Visual / Direction | Narration / Audio | On-screen Text |

|---|---|---|---|

| 0:00–0:06 | Close-up of a person sitting quietly. Shoulders slightly tense. Slow camera movement. | Stress is not just in your mind. It also shows up in your body. | Stress affects the body |

| 0:06–0:16 | Simple animation: “alarm mode” icon appears. Heartbeat line speeds up. Muscles gently highlighted. | When you feel stressed, your nervous system can shift into fight-or-flight mode. Your heart may beat faster, your muscles may tighten, and your body prepares to react. | Fight-or-flight = alarm mode |

| 0:16–0:26 | Animation of lungs expanding and softening. A hand moves from chest to belly. | Deep breathing helps because breathing is one of the few body functions that happens automatically — but can also be gently guided. | Breathing can be gently guided |

| 0:42–0:58 | Animation shifts from “alarm” to “recovery” using color, labels, and icons: alarm bell lowers, recovery icon appears, movement slows. | Think of the vagus nerve like a communication line between your body and brain. | 0:58–1:08 | Person’s shoulders soften. Breathing slows. Heartbeat animation steadies. | In simple terms: slow, comfortable breathing can help your body move out of high-alert mode and toward a steadier state. | Slow breath sends a calmer signal |

| 1:08–1:22 | Clean animated icons: alarm dial lowering, heart rhythm becoming steadier and more flexible, stress meter easing down. Keep text minimal. | Research connects slow, diaphragmatic breathing with reduced sympathetic arousal — less fight-or-flight activation — improved heart-rate variability, and lower perceived stress or anxiety. | Less alarm activation<br>Lower perceived stress |

| 1:48–1:58 | Timer shifts to exhale. Person exhales slowly; shoulders remain relaxed. | Now exhale slowly for about six seconds, through your nose or mouth. Let your body soften as the air leaves. | Exhale slowly<br>1… 2… 3… 4… 5… 6 |
