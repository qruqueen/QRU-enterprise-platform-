Step 4

Simple Answer

High blood pressure is usually detected through repeated, properly taken blood pressure readings, not by symptoms, because many people feel completely normal. It can often be controlled with a combination of healthy habits, regular monitoring, medical follow-up, and medications when needed.

Step 5

Why It Matters

High blood pressure, also called hypertension, quietly increases the risk of:

  • Heart attack
  • Stroke
  • Heart failure
  • Kidney disease
  • Vision problems
  • Blood vessel damage
  • Premature death

The good news: controlling blood pressure can substantially reduce these risks and protect long-term health.

Step 6

QRU Translation™

Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against artery walls. High blood pressure means that force is consistently higher than it should be.

Under many current U.S. guidelines, hypertension is often defined as an average systolic blood pressure of 130 mm Hg or higher or an average diastolic blood pressure of 80 mm Hg or higher.

However, diagnosis is usually based on repeated readings over time, not one isolated number. When possible, clinicians often confirm high blood pressure with home blood pressure monitoring or ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, which measures blood pressure over many hours during normal daily life.

Blood pressure categories and treatment decisions can vary depending on:

  • Country or regional guidelines
  • Age
  • Pregnancy status
  • Kidney disease
  • Diabetes
  • Existing heart or blood vessel disease
  • Overall cardiovascular risk
  • Medication side effects or tolerance
  • Clinician judgment

The core idea is simple: measure accurately, confirm patterns, and manage risk.

Step 7

Everyday Analogy

High blood pressure is like water pressure running too high through a house’s pipes. You may not notice a problem right away, but over time the extra force can damage the system.

Your arteries, heart, brain, kidneys, and eyes can all be affected by that long-term pressure.

Step 8

What the Numbers Mean

A blood pressure reading has two numbers:

Example: 128/78 mm Hg

| Number | Name | What It Means |

|---|---|---|

| 128 | Systolic pressure | The pressure when the heart squeezes and pumps blood |

| 78 | Diastolic pressure | The pressure when the heart relaxes between beats |

The unit mm Hg means “millimeters of mercury,” the traditional unit used to measure blood pressure.

Step 9

Common Blood Pressure Categories

The following categories reflect commonly used U.S. adult guidelines. Your clinician may use different thresholds depending on your health situation and local guidance.

| Category | Systolic | Diastolic |

|---|---:|---:|

| Normal | Less than 120 | And less than 80 |

| Elevated | 120–129 | And less than 80 |

| Hypertension Stage 1 | 130–139 | Or 80–89 |

| Hypertension Stage 2 | 140 or higher | Or 90 or higher |

| Hypertensive crisis range | Around 180 or higher | And/or around 120 or higher |

Important: A single elevated reading usually does not mean you have hypertension. Diagnosis is typically based on the average of repeated, properly measured readings, often confirmed outside the clinic.

Step 11

How to Measure Blood Pressure Correctly

Accurate blood pressure measurement matters. A rushed, poorly positioned, or improperly taken reading can look higher or lower than it really is.

Before Measuring

For the most accurate reading:

  • Avoid caffeine for at least 30 minutes beforehand.
  • Avoid exercise for at least 30 minutes beforehand.
  • Avoid smoking or nicotine for at least 30 minutes beforehand.
  • Empty your bladder first.
  • Sit quietly and rest for 5 minutes before measuring.
  • Use a validated blood pressure monitor when possible.
  • Use the correct cuff size for your arm.

Correct Body Position

When measuring:

  • Sit in a chair with your back supported.
  • Keep both feet flat on the floor.
  • Do not cross your legs.
  • Support your arm on a table or surface.
  • Keep the cuffed arm at heart level.
  • Place the cuff on bare skin, not over clothing.
  • Stay still.
  • Do not talk during the reading.

Taking Multiple Readings

Because blood pressure naturally changes throughout the day:

1. Take two readings, at least 1 minute apart.

2. Record both numbers.

3. If instructed by your clinician, measure at the same times each day, such as morning and evening.

4. Use the average of repeated readings rather than focusing on one number.

Home Monitoring Tip

Home monitoring is often helpful because clinic readings can be affected by stress, rushing, pain, caffeine, or “white coat” effects. Some people also have normal clinic readings but high readings at home, known as masked hypertension.
