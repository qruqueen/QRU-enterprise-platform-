Step 2

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Explain what hope means during hardship beyond “positive thinking.”

2. Describe how hope can support coping, motivation, resilience, meaning, treatment engagement, and social connection.

3. Recognize that hope can coexist with fear, sadness, anger, uncertainty, trauma, illness, or grief.

4. Distinguish realistic hope from toxic positivity.

5. Practice simple steps for strengthening hope in a compassionate, practical way.

Step 3

Simple Answer

Hope helps people endure hardship by supporting motivation, coping, resilience, and the belief that there may be workable paths forward.

Hope does not erase suffering, deny grief, or guarantee success. Instead, it helps people keep going, stay connected to meaning and support, adapt when plans change, and take one possible next step.

Step 4

Safety Note: Hope Is Not Pressure

Hope should never be used to pressure someone to “stay positive,” minimize pain, dismiss grief, avoid medical or mental health treatment, or blame them for suffering.

A person can have hope and still feel afraid, sad, angry, exhausted, numb, uncertain, or heartbroken. Those feelings are not failures. They are human responses to hardship.

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, at risk of self-harm, thinking about suicide, unable to stay safe, or experiencing a mental health crisis, seek urgent help now. Contact local emergency services, go to the nearest emergency department if available, call a crisis hotline in your country, or reach out to a trusted medical professional or safe person nearby. If you are outside the United States, use your local emergency number or a recognized crisis support service in your region.

Step 5

Why It Matters

Hardship can make people feel trapped, powerless, or alone. Hope matters because it can help restore a sense of possibility.

Hope is not pretending everything is fine. It is a practical psychological resource that can help people:

  • Identify something meaningful to move toward
  • Look for possible pathways forward
  • Keep trying when the first plan fails
  • Ask for support
  • Stay engaged with treatment, recovery, relationships, or daily life
  • Notice small signs of progress
  • Reconnect with meaning, values, faith, community, or purpose

Hope does not remove the storm, but it can help people find a next step inside it.

Step 6

QRU Translation™

Hope is more than positive thinking.

In psychological and health research, hope is often understood as having three connected parts:

1. Goals — something meaningful you want or need.

2. Pathways — possible ways to move toward that goal.

3. Agency — the motivation, willingness, or support to keep trying, adjust, or ask for help.

This means hope is not just a feeling. It is connected to how people think, plan, cope, adapt, and reach for support.

Research has associated higher hope with better psychological adjustment during hardship, including stronger coping, greater resilience, reduced distress, and increased motivation to pursue meaningful goals. Hope can also support treatment engagement, social connection, and meaning-making, especially when people face illness, grief, trauma, loss, uncertainty, or major life disruption.

But hope is not magic. It does not remove pain. It does not promise a perfect outcome. It helps people keep relating to life as if something meaningful may still be possible.

Step 7

Hope Is Not the Same as Certainty

Hope does not require knowing the outcome.

A person can say:

  • “I do not know what will happen, but I can take the next step.”
  • “This hurts, and I still want support.”
  • “I am grieving, and I can still care for myself today.”
  • “The path has changed, but maybe there is another path.”
  • “I cannot fix everything, but I can do one meaningful thing.”

That is realistic hope.

Step 8

Realistic Hope vs. Toxic Positivity

| Realistic Hope | Toxic Positivity |

|---|---|

| Makes room for pain and possibility | Pressures people to ignore pain |

| Says, “This is hard, and we can look for a next step” | Says, “Just be positive” |

| Allows grief, anger, fear, and uncertainty | Treats difficult emotions as failure |

| Encourages support and wise action | Encourages emotional suppression |

| Adapts when plans change | Pretends everything will work out perfectly |

| Honors reality | Avoids reality |

Key QRU Principle:

Hope becomes harmful when it is used to silence suffering. Hope becomes healing when it helps people face reality with support, meaning, and one possible next step.

Step 9

Hope Can Look Different for Different People

Hope is personal and culturally shaped. It may look different across:

  • Cultures and communities
  • Faith traditions and spiritual beliefs
  • Family systems
  • Health situations
  • Trauma histories
  • Stages of grief
  • Experiences of discrimination, displacement, poverty, illness, or loss
  • Individual personality and coping style

For one person, hope may sound like prayer. For another, it may be medical planning. For another, it may be community care, rest, activism, grief rituals, therapy, creative expression, or simply surviving one more day.

There is no single “correct” way for hope to look.

Step 10

Everyday Analogy

Hope is like having a flashlight and a flexible map during a dark storm.

The flashlight does not stop the storm. The map may need to change. But together, they help you see one possible next step instead of standing still in the dark.

Step 11

Real-World Example: Illness

A person going through cancer treatment may feel afraid, exhausted, angry, and uncertain.

Hope does not mean they are sure everything will turn out well. It may mean they can still focus on meaningful goals, such as:

  • Attending the next treatment appointment
  • Asking the doctor clearer questions
  • Managing symptoms as well as possible
  • Spending time with family or friends
  • Resting without shame
  • Adjusting plans when the situation changes

Their hope does not deny the seriousness of what they are facing. It helps them stay connected to care, support, and meaning while facing it.

Step 12

Real-World Example: Grief

A person grieving the death of someone they love may not feel hopeful in a bright or cheerful way.

Hope may look like:

  • Getting through one morning
  • Letting someone sit with them
  • Lighting a candle or practicing a ritual
  • Crying without apologizing
  • Eating something small
  • Remembering that love can remain even when life has changed
  • Believing that grief may become more bearable over time

In grief, hope often begins gently. It may not feel like happiness. It may feel like being held, breathing again, or surviving the next hour.

Step 13

Real-World Example: Job Loss

A person who loses a job may feel ashamed, scared, angry, or overwhelmed.

Hope may look like:

  • Updating one section of a résumé
  • Calling one trusted friend
  • Applying for one benefit, resource, or support program
  • Taking a walk to regulate stress
  • Making a list of transferable skills
  • Remembering, “My job loss is real, but it is not the whole story of who I am”

Hope does not remove the financial pressure. It helps the person look for possible pathways through it.

Use the HOPE acronym to strengthen realistic hope without denying reality.

Step 14

H — Honor What Is Hard

Name the truth compassionately.

Examples:

  • “This is painful.”
  • “I feel scared and uncertain.”
  • “I am exhausted.”
  • “I do not know what happens next.”
  • “I need support.”

Hope begins with honesty, not denial.

Step 15

O — Orient Toward Meaning

Identify something that still matters.

Ask:

  • What do I care about right now?
  • Who or what matters to me?
  • What value do I want to live by in this moment?
  • What would help me feel slightly more grounded?
  • What is one meaningful goal, even if it is small?

Examples of meaningful goals:

  • Resting enough to make a decision
  • Asking for help
  • Attending an appointment
  • Protecting my safety
  • Caring for my child
  • Staying connected to my faith or community
  • Getting through today
Step 16

P — Plan One Possible Pathway

Hope grows when there is at least one possible route forward.

Ask:

  • What is one small step I could take?
  • Who has information that could help?
  • What resource might support me?
  • What is another option if the first plan does not work?
  • What can I do today, not forever?

A pathway does not need to solve the whole problem. It only needs to make the next step more visible.

Step 17

E — Engage Support and Take One Next Step

Hope is not always something we create alone.

Ask:

  • Who can I tell the truth to?
  • Who can help me think clearly?
  • What professional, community, spiritual, or medical support might help?
  • What is one action I can take in the next 10 minutes, hour, or day?

Examples:

  • Send a message: “I am having a hard day. Can you check in?”
  • Call a clinic, counselor, mentor, crisis line, or support service
  • Write down three questions for a doctor or advisor
  • Drink water, eat something, or rest
  • Take one practical step toward a meaningful goal

Guided Response

Step 25

Strengthen Realistic Hope in 10 Minutes

Use this activity when you feel overwhelmed, discouraged, or uncertain.

1. Name the hardship.

> “Right now, I am facing…”

Keep it honest and specific.

2. Name one meaningful goal.

> “One thing that matters to me is…”

This does not need to be big. It may be safety, rest, clarity, connection, treatment, stability, dignity, or getting through today.

3. Identify one possible pathway.

> “One possible way forward is…”

If you cannot think of a pathway, try:

> “One person who may help me find a pathway is…”

4. Ask for support.

> “The support I need or could request is…”

Support may include:

  • A friend, family member, mentor, elder, or community member
  • A therapist, doctor, nurse, social worker, teacher, coach, or spiritual leader
  • A crisis line or emergency service if safety is at risk
  • A support group or community organization
  • Practical help such as childcare, transportation, food, rest, or information

5. Choose one next action.

> “My next action is…”

Make it small enough to do.

Examples:

  • Send one message
  • Make one phone call
  • Drink water
  • Write down questions
  • Schedule an appointment
  • Step outside for three minutes
  • Ask someone to sit with you
  • Put one task on tomorrow’s list
  • Rest without calling it failure

Key Takeaways

  • Toxic positivity pressures people to perform happiness. Realistic hope gives people room to be human.
  • One small next step can be a real expression of hope.

Hope sustains people through hardship by helping them stay connected to meaning, support, and possibility when life feels painful or uncertain.

Hope is not forced cheerfulness. It is not denial. It is not a demand to feel better before you are ready.

Realistic hope says:

> “This is hard. My feelings make sense. I do not know everything that will happen. But something meaningful may still be possible, and I can take one supported next step.”
