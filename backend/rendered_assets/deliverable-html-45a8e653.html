Introduction

AI literacy is the set of knowledge, skills, and dispositions that enables people to understand, evaluate, use, and participate in decisions about artificial intelligence systems.

2

Why It Matters

AI is increasingly embedded in education, work, healthcare, media, and public services. AI literacy helps people use AI tools with confidence while recognizing their limits, making safer choices about privacy, bias, accuracy, transparency, accountability, and human oversight. It also supports informed citizenship, fair participation, safer adoption, and more equitable outcomes.

3

How It Works

AI literacy combines practical capability with responsible judgment. Practically, it includes recognizing where AI is being used, understanding that AI systems have limitations, and evaluating outputs critically. Responsibly, it includes asking questions about privacy, bias, transparency, accountability, and when human oversight is needed. The most useful AI literacy education combines technical understanding, ethical reasoning, critical evaluation of AI outputs, and hands-on practice.

4

The Core Idea

AI literacy is like learning to drive a powerful car: you do not need to build the engine, but you do need to understand the controls, the limits, the safety rules, and when the human driver must pay close attention.

5

An Analogy

Using AI without AI literacy is like driving a powerful car without understanding the dashboard, road rules, blind spots, or when to slow down. AI literacy does not require you to design the engine, but it does require you to operate the tool thoughtfully and stay responsible for decisions.

6

A Story

A student uses a generative AI tool to draft a study guide. Because the student has AI literacy, they do not simply accept the draft as correct. They check whether the information is accurate, notice whether important perspectives may be missing, avoid entering private personal data, and use human judgment before relying on the final study guide.

7

Key Terms

AI literacy — Knowledge, skills, and dispositions that help people understand, evaluate, use, and participate in decisions about AI systems.

Artificial intelligence system — A system referred to in the provided knowledge as AI; it may be used in areas such as education, work, healthcare, media, and public services.

Critical evaluation — Carefully checking AI outputs instead of accepting them automatically.

Privacy — A responsibility area involving caution about personal or sensitive information when using AI.

Bias — A responsibility area involving the possibility that AI outputs or decisions may reflect unfair or limited perspectives.

Transparency — A responsibility area involving the need to understand or question how AI is being used and what is visible about that use.

Accountability — A responsibility area involving who is responsible for AI use, outputs, or decisions.

Human oversight — Human attention and judgment applied before relying on AI outputs or decisions.

Hands-on practice — Learning AI literacy by actually using AI tools while evaluating their outputs and limits.

Common Misconceptions

AI literacy means knowing how to build AI systems. —

AI literacy is only a technical skill. —

If an AI tool sounds confident, its output can be trusted. —

AI literacy is only important for schools or technology jobs. —

Responsible AI use only means getting useful results. —

9

Putting It Into Practice

  • Checking the accuracy of an AI-generated study guide before using it
  • Avoiding entry of private personal data into an AI tool
  • Recognizing when AI may be involved in education, work, healthcare, media, or public services
  • Questioning whether an AI output may leave out important perspectives
  • Using human judgment before relying on an AI-generated answer
  • Participating in discussions or decisions about how AI should be used responsibly
