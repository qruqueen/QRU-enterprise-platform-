Step 2

Learning Objectives

By the end of this interactive lesson, you will be able to:

1. Explain the basic physical activity targets for adults, children, and adolescents.

2. Tell the difference between moderate and vigorous activity using practical cues like the talk test.

3. Identify examples of aerobic, muscle-strengthening, and bone-strengthening activities.

4. Create a realistic weekly activity plan that fits your ability, schedule, and environment.

5. Recognize warning symptoms that mean you should stop activity and seek medical guidance.

6. Apply the memory formula: Move most days, strengthen twice weekly, start small, progress gradually.

Step 3

1. Simple Answer

For most adults, the weekly goal is:

  • 150–300 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, or
  • 75–150 minutes per week of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity, or
  • An equivalent mix of moderate and vigorous activity

plus

  • Muscle-strengthening activities at least 2 days per week

For children and adolescents ages 6–17, the general goal is:

  • An average of at least 60 minutes per day of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity
  • Include vigorous activity, muscle-strengthening activity, and bone-strengthening activity at least 3 days per week

For inactive beginners, remember this important truth:

> Some physical activity is better than none. Health benefits begin even below the target amounts.

You do not need to reach the full guideline immediately. The safest and most sustainable approach is to begin where you are, then build gradually.

Step 4

2. QRU Translation™

Physical activity is one of the most powerful daily habits for long-term health.

For adults, the basic target is regular aerobic movement such as walking, cycling, swimming, dancing, or active chores, combined with strengthening work at least twice each week.

For children and teens, growing bodies need frequent movement, so the target is higher: about an hour a day on average, with a mix of activities that raise the heart rate, strengthen muscles, and load the bones in healthy ways.

If you are inactive, recovering from injury, pregnant, older, disabled, or living with a chronic condition, the goal is not to “push through.” The goal is to choose appropriate movement, start small, progress gradually, and seek medical guidance when symptoms or health concerns suggest it is needed.

Step 5

3. Memory Formula™

> Move most days, strengthen twice weekly, start small, progress gradually.

Use this as your simple lifelong health formula:

  • Move most days: Build regular aerobic activity into your week.
  • Strengthen twice weekly: Train major muscle groups at least 2 days per week.
  • Start small: Especially if you have been inactive.
  • Progress gradually: Increase time, frequency, or intensity step by step.
Step 6

4. Why Physical Activity Matters

Regular physical activity is linked with lower risk of:

  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Type 2 diabetes
  • Some cancers
  • Depression and anxiety symptoms
  • Cognitive decline
  • Falls and fall-related injuries
  • Premature death

It also supports:

  • Better sleep
  • Stronger bones and muscles
  • Improved balance and coordination
  • Healthier blood pressure and blood sugar control
  • Better mood and energy
  • Greater independence with daily activities

Physical activity is not only about athletic fitness. It is about helping your body, brain, and daily life function better over time.

Step 7

5. Everyday Analogy

Physical activity is like charging a battery.

Small, regular charges keep the whole system working better than waiting until the battery is nearly empty and trying to fix everything at once.

A 10-minute walk, a few chair exercises, a short dance break, or a gentle stretching routine may seem small, but repeated consistently, these “charges” add up.

Step 8

6. Intensity Made Simple: The Talk Test

You do not need fancy equipment to estimate intensity. Use the talk test.

| Intensity | What It Feels Like | Talk Test Cue | Examples |

|---|---|---|---|

| Light | Easy movement; breathing is normal or slightly increased | You can talk and sing | Slow walking, gentle stretching, light household tasks |

| Moderate | Breathing and heart rate increase, but you still feel in control | You can talk, but not sing | Brisk walking, easy cycling, water aerobics, active gardening |

| Vigorous | Breathing is much harder; effort feels challenging | Speaking more than a few words is difficult | Running, fast cycling, lap swimming, singles tennis, uphill hiking |

Key Point

Moderate activity is not “all out.” It is effortful but manageable.

Vigorous activity is harder and may not be appropriate for everyone, especially beginners or people with certain health conditions, unless they have been medically cleared or have built up gradually.
